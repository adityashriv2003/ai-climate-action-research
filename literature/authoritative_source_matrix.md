# Authoritative literature matrix: AI for climate mitigation and adaptation

Prepared 2026-09-06. The labels below deliberately separate observed/operational evidence from hindcast benchmarks, life-cycle or scenario models, reviews, and normative arguments. A model-performance gain is not, by itself, an avoided-emissions or avoided-loss outcome.

## Cross-cutting frameworks

1. **David Rolnick; Priya L. Donti; Lynn H. Kaack; Kelly Kochanski; Alexandre Lacoste; Kris Sankaran; Andrew Slavin Ross; Nikola Milojevic-Dupont; Natasha Jaques; Anna Waldman-Brown; Alexandra Sasha Luccioni; Tegan Maharaj; Evan D. Sherwin; S. Karthik Mukkavilli; Konrad P. Kording; Carla P. Gomes; Andrew Y. Ng; Demis Hassabis; John C. Platt; Felix Creutzig; Jennifer Chayes; Yoshua Bengio.** “Tackling Climate Change with Machine Learning.” *ACM Computing Surveys* 55(2), Article 42, 1–96 (2022). [DOI 10.1145/3485128](https://doi.org/10.1145/3485128). **Evidence type:** expert-informed review/taxonomy. **Supports:** an authoritative map of ML opportunities in electricity, transport, buildings, industry, land use, carbon removal, climate prediction, and adaptation. **Boundary:** opportunity identification, not an estimate of realized abatement; the paper treats ML as an enabling tool that requires domain collaboration and policy.

2. **Lynn H. Kaack; Priya L. Donti; Emma Strubell; George Kamiya; Felix Creutzig; David Rolnick.** “Aligning artificial intelligence with climate change mitigation.” *Nature Climate Change* 12, 518–527 (2022). [DOI 10.1038/s41558-022-01377-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-022-01377-7). **Evidence type:** peer-reviewed assessment framework. **Supports:** AI’s GHG effects should be separated into computing-related effects, immediate application effects, and economy/system-level effects; evaluation should cover the full model life cycle and structural rebound. **Boundary:** framework and policy analysis, not a causal abatement estimate.

3. **Josh Cowls; Andreas Tsamados; Mariarosaria Taddeo; Luciano Floridi.** “The AI gambit: leveraging artificial intelligence to combat climate change—opportunities, challenges, and recommendations.” *AI & Society* 38, 283–307 (2023; first published online 2021). [DOI 10.1007/s00146-021-01294-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-021-01294-x). **Evidence type:** peer-reviewed conceptual review. **Supports:** AI can improve climate understanding and interventions, but its own emissions and social/ethical risks require responsive, evidence-based governance; the paper gives 13 recommendations. **Boundary:** recommendations and synthesis, not an intervention trial.

4. **Ricardo Vinuesa; Hossein Azizpour; Iolanda Leite; Madeline Balaam; Virginia Dignum; Sami Domisch; Anna Felländer; Simone Daniela Langhans; Max Tegmark; Francesco Fuso Nerini.** “The role of artificial intelligence in achieving the Sustainable Development Goals.” *Nature Communications* 11, 233 (2020). [DOI 10.1038/s41467-019-14108-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-14108-y). **Evidence type:** consensus-based expert elicitation informed by literature. **Supports:** AI may enable 134 of 169 SDG targets and inhibit 59 (a target can appear in both sets), motivating regulatory insight and oversight. **Boundary:** “may enable/inhibit” is potential, not demonstrated causality; many underlying studies were prototypes or limited-data studies.

## Electricity, energy systems, buildings, and industry

5. **Priya L. Donti; J. Zico Kolter.** “Machine Learning for Sustainable Energy Systems.” *Annual Review of Environment and Resources* 46, 719–747 (2021). [DOI 10.1146/annurev-environ-020220-061831](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-environ-020220-061831). **Evidence type:** peer-reviewed review. **Supports:** ML can assist renewable/load forecasting, power-system optimization and control, storage, and accelerated materials/science; deployment needs physics integration, robustness, auditability, and attention to distribution shift and unequal data access. **Boundary:** synthesis of heterogeneous studies; no single aggregate emissions effect.

6. **José R. Vázquez-Canteli; Zoltán Nagy.** “Reinforcement learning for demand response: A review of algorithms and modeling techniques.” *Applied Energy* 235, 1072–1089 (2019). [DOI 10.1016/j.apenergy.2018.11.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apenergy.2018.11.002). **Evidence type:** peer-reviewed review. **Supports:** RL can control HVAC, appliances, batteries, and EV loads for demand response and renewable integration; comfort feedback and multi-agent coordination are key research needs. **Boundary:** much of the reviewed evidence is simulated or single-agent/stationary; shifting a peak rather than shaving it is a documented risk under demand-dependent prices.

7. **Hussain Kazmi; Fahad Mehmood; Stefan Lodeweyckx; Johan Driesen.** “Gigawatt-hour scale savings on a budget of zero: Deep reinforcement learning based optimal control of hot water systems.” *Energy* 144, 159–168 (2018). [DOI 10.1016/j.energy.2017.12.019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.energy.2017.12.019). **Evidence type:** real-house field application. **Supports:** in 32 Dutch houses, the controller reduced hot-water energy use by roughly 20%; no comfort loss was observed in the five houses equipped with extra comfort sensors; the authors extrapolated roughly 200 kWh per household-year. **Boundary:** context-specific field evidence, not a randomized trial or universal building/HVAC effect; annual and gigawatt-hour figures are extrapolations.

8. **International Energy Agency (IEA).** *Energy and AI.* Paris: IEA (2025). [Report](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai); [energy-demand chapter](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-demand-from-ai); [AI-and-climate chapter](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/ai-and-climate-change); [optimization chapter](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/ai-for-energy-optimisation-and-innovation). **Evidence type:** official scenario analysis informed by case studies. **Supports:** data centres used about 415 TWh (1.5% of global electricity) in 2024; the Base Case reaches about 945 TWh in 2030. In the expressly non-forecast “Widespread Adoption Case,” existing AI applications yield 1,400 Mt CO2 of potential 2035 reductions, including examples such as 5–10% vehicle-operation efficiency and about 10% HVAC energy savings; up to 175 GW of existing transmission capacity and 8% light-industry energy savings are modeled for 2035. **Boundary:** these are scenario-scaled potentials contingent on adoption and removal of barriers, not observed global savings; rebound can erode them.

## Transport

9. **Zia Wadud; Don MacKenzie; Paul Leiby.** “Help or hindrance? The travel, energy and carbon impacts of highly automated vehicles.” *Transportation Research Part A: Policy and Practice* 86, 1–18 (2016). [DOI 10.1016/j.tra.2015.12.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tra.2015.12.001). **Evidence type:** decomposition/scenario model plus literature review. **Supports:** automation could plausibly reduce road-transport energy/GHG by nearly half or nearly double them, depending on vehicle efficiency, induced travel, design, and fuel effects; it is a strong rebound warning. **Boundary:** illustrative U.S.-focused scenarios, not a fleet observation or forecast.

10. **James H. Gawron; Gregory A. Keoleian; Robert D. De Kleine; Timothy J. Wallington; Hyung Chul Kim.** “Life Cycle Assessment of Connected and Automated Vehicles: Sensing and Computing Subsystem and Vehicle Level Effects.” *Environmental Science & Technology* 52(5), 3249–3256 (2018). [DOI 10.1021/acs.est.7b04576](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.7b04576). **Evidence type:** prospective life-cycle assessment/model. **Supports:** sensing/computing, added mass, drag, power, and data transmission could raise primary energy/GHG 3–20%; after assumed eco-driving, platooning, and intersection benefits, the base case gives up to 9% net reduction. **Boundary:** results depend on prospective technology and operational assumptions, not observed fleet emissions.

## Agriculture, forests, land use, and measurement/reporting/verification (MRV)

11. **Andreas Kamilaris; Francesc X. Prenafeta-Boldú.** “Deep learning in agriculture: A survey.” *Computers and Electronics in Agriculture* 147, 70–90 (2018). [DOI 10.1016/j.compag.2018.02.016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compag.2018.02.016). **Evidence type:** review of 40 studies. **Supports:** deep learning has been applied to crop/land/food image classification and prediction and often outperformed conventional image-processing methods under matched study conditions, enabling precision management and monitoring. **Boundary:** task accuracy is not evidence of lower fertilizer, water, land, or GHG use.

12. **M.C. Hansen; P.V. Potapov; R. Moore; M. Hancher; S.A. Turubanova; A. Tyukavina; D. Thau; S.V. Stehman; S.J. Goetz; T.R. Loveland; A. Kommareddy; A. Egorov; L. Chini; C.O. Justice; J.R.G. Townshend.** “High-Resolution Global Maps of 21st-Century Forest Cover Change.” *Science* 342(6160), 850–853 (2013). [DOI 10.1126/science.1244693](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1244693); [USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70048671). **Evidence type:** empirical automated satellite analysis. **Supports:** global Landsat analysis mapped 2000–2012 forest loss (2.3 million km²) and gain (0.8 million km²) at 30 m, demonstrating globally consistent, locally relevant land-change MRV. **Boundary:** detection/monitoring alone does not demonstrate reduced deforestation or accurate carbon stock without additional models/ground truth.

13. **Martin Brandt; Compton J. Tucker; Ankit Kariryaa; Kjeld Rasmussen; Christin Abel; Jennifer Small; Jérôme Chave; Laura Vang Rasmussen; Pierre Hiernaux; Abdoul Aziz Diouf; Laurent Kergoat; Ole Mertz; Christian Igel; Fabian Gieseke; Johannes Schöning; Sizhuo Li; Katherine Melocik; Jesse Meyer; Scott Sinno; Eric Romero; Erin Glennie; Amandine Montagu; Morgane Dendoncker; Rasmus Fensholt.** “An unexpectedly large count of trees in the West African Sahara and Sahel.” *Nature* 587, 78–82 (2020). [DOI 10.1038/s41586-020-2824-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2824-5). **Evidence type:** empirical deep-learning remote sensing. **Supports:** sub-metre imagery plus deep learning mapped crowns of more than 1.8 billion individual trees over 1.3 million km², including non-forest drylands poorly represented in conventional maps; this supports granular ecosystem/carbon inventory. **Boundary:** tree detection is not itself a carbon-stock estimate or mitigation outcome.

14. **United Nations Environment Programme, International Methane Emissions Observatory (UNEP-IMEO).** “Methane Alert and Response System (MARS).” Operational webpage (system launched 2022; page updated 2026). [System page](https://www.unep.org/topics/energy/methane/methane-alert-and-response-system-mars); [methodology](https://methanedata.unep.org/mars-approach-methodology); [mitigation cases](https://www.unep.org/topics/energy/methane/mars-mitigation-action). **Evidence type:** official operational system and documented case follow-up. **Supports:** MARS is a public global satellite detection/notification system using data from more than 30 satellite instruments, scientific expertise, and advanced AI to alert governments and firms; UNEP reports more than 40 mitigation cases as of April 2026 and verified/catalysed action across four continents. **Boundary:** official case documentation is not peer-reviewed counterfactual attribution or an aggregate avoided-emissions estimate; UNEP says responses have not kept pace with notifications.

## Climate and extreme-weather modeling

15. **Markus Reichstein; Gustau Camps-Valls; Bjorn Stevens; Martin Jung; Joachim Denzler; Nuno Carvalhais; Prabhat.** “Deep learning and process understanding for data-driven Earth system science.” *Nature* 566, 195–204 (2019). [DOI 10.1038/s41586-019-0912-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-0912-1). **Evidence type:** peer-reviewed perspective/synthesis. **Supports:** hybrid physics–ML models can exploit spatiotemporal context, improve seasonal prediction and long-range dependence modeling, and potentially increase process understanding. **Boundary:** agenda and methodological argument, not direct abatement or adaptation-outcome evidence.

16. **Dmitrii Kochkov; Janni Yuval; Ian Langmore; Peter Norgaard; Jamie Smith; Griffin Mooers; Milan Klöwer; James Lottes; Stephan Rasp; Peter Düben; Sam Hatfield; Peter Battaglia; Alvaro Sanchez-Gonzalez; Matthew Willson; Michael P. Brenner; Stephan Hoyer.** “Neural general circulation models for weather and climate.” *Nature* 632, 1060–1066 (2024). [DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07744-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07744-y). **Evidence type:** computational forecasts/hindcasts and prescribed-SST climate simulations. **Supports:** NeuralGCM was competitive for 1–10-day deterministic and 1–15-day ensemble forecasts, remained stable in selected multidecadal simulations, and used 3–5 orders of magnitude fewer computational resources than selected conventional comparators. **Boundary:** it underestimates tropical extremes, only 22/37 selected 40-year runs were stable, and it does not extrapolate to substantially different future climates; it is not yet a complete coupled future-climate projection system.

17. **Suman Ravuri; Karel Lenc; Matthew Willson; Dmitry Kangin; Remi Lam; Piotr Mirowski; Megan Fitzsimons; Maria Athanassiadou; Sheleem Kashem; Sam Madge; Rachel Prudden; Amol Mandhane; Aidan Clark; Andrew Brock; Karen Simonyan; Raia Hadsell; Niall Robinson; Ellen Clancy; Alberto Arribas; Shakir Mohamed.** “Skilful precipitation nowcasting using deep generative models of radar.” *Nature* 597, 672–677 (2021). [DOI 10.1038/s41586-021-03854-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03854-z). **Evidence type:** held-out radar benchmark plus blinded expert evaluation. **Supports:** for 0–2-hour nowcasting, DGMR significantly beat PySTEPS on critical-success-index tests; 56 meteorologists most preferred it 89% and 90% of the time for medium- and heavy-rain cases, respectively; U.S. data provided an additional generalization test. **Boundary:** heavy precipitation at long lead times remained difficult; forecast skill is not an avoided-loss outcome and operational warning integration is still required.

18. **Ilan Price; Alvaro Sanchez-Gonzalez; Ferran Alet; Tom R. Andersson; Andrew El-Kadi; Dominic Masters; Timo Ewalds; Jacklynn Stott; Shakir Mohamed; Peter Battaglia; Remi Lam; Matthew Willson.** “Probabilistic weather forecasting with machine learning.” *Nature* 637, 84–90 (2025; published online 2024). [DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-08252-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08252-9). **Evidence type:** 2019 hindcast benchmark. **Supports:** GenCast produced 15-day, 0.25° ensemble forecasts in about eight minutes and significantly outperformed ECMWF ENS on 97.2% of 1,320 evaluated targets, with stronger evaluated performance for several extremes, cyclone tracks, and wind-power applications. **Boundary:** it was trained/initialized on reanalysis and evaluated against model-specific analyses; precipitation was excluded from main scorecards; forecast skill does not itself prove climate adaptation or replace official services.

## Adaptation, disaster-risk management, and decision systems

19. **Amir Mosavi; Pinar Ozturk; Kwok-wing Chau.** “Flood Prediction Using Machine Learning Models: Literature Review.” *Water* 10(11), 1536 (2018). [DOI 10.3390/w10111536](https://doi.org/10.3390/w10111536). **Evidence type:** review (180 retained original studies from 6,596 search results). **Supports:** ML has been widely applied to short- and long-term flood prediction; hybridization, decomposition, ensembles, and optimization are recurring routes to improved accuracy/lead time. **Boundary:** highly heterogeneous datasets, baselines, and metrics; the review does not establish one universally superior algorithm or avoided-damage outcome.

20. **World Meteorological Organization (WMO).** *Early Warnings for All: Executive Action Plan 2023–2027.* Geneva: WMO, 50 pp. (2022). [UN Digital Library record](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4001006). **Evidence type:** official implementation plan. **Supports:** useful early warning is an end-to-end, people-centred system comprising risk knowledge, detection/observation/forecasting, dissemination/communication, and preparedness/response; AI forecast advances must connect to institutions and last-mile action. **Boundary:** policy/implementation architecture, not an AI-effectiveness trial.

21. **IPCC.** *Climate Change 2022: Impacts, Adaptation and Vulnerability. Contribution of Working Group II to the Sixth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change* [H.-O. Pörtner; D.C. Roberts; M. Tignor; E.S. Poloczanska; K. Mintenbeck; A. Alegría; M. Craig; S. Langsdorf; S. Löschke; V. Möller; A. Okem; B. Rama, eds.]. Cambridge University Press (2022). [DOI 10.1017/9781009325844](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009325844); [official report page](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/). **Evidence type:** official global assessment. **Supports:** reliable climate services, monitoring, and early-warning systems are the most common strategies for key risks (high confidence); services work better when decision-specific and co-produced, and integrated tools that accommodate community values/multiple knowledge systems are more effective than narrow single-risk tools. **Boundary:** supports design/implementation conditions; it does not attribute these benefits specifically to AI.

22. **Sara Mehryar; Vahid Yazdanpanah; Jeffrey Tong.** “AI and climate resilience governance.” *iScience* 27(6), 109812 (2024). [DOI 10.1016/j.isci.2024.109812](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2024.109812); [open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11112607/). **Evidence type:** systematic review of 244 application studies plus survey of 40 experts. **Supports:** 64% of reviewed studies focused only on risk/resilience assessment, versus 29% on policy appraisal and 6% on implementation; recurring gaps include sparse socioeconomic/vulnerability data, long-term dynamics, data bias/quality, interpretability, skills, and human oversight. **Boundary:** included multiple publication types and only studies explicitly mentioning AI; expert survey was purposive and mostly academic.

## Governance, rebound, and justice

23. **IPCC.** *Climate Change 2022: Mitigation of Climate Change. Contribution of Working Group III to the Sixth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change* [P.R. Shukla; J. Skea; R. Slade; A. Al Khourdajie; R. van Diemen; D. McCollum; M. Pathak; S. Some; P. Vyas; R. Fradera; M. Belkacemi; A. Hasija; G. Lisboa; S. Luz; J. Malley, eds.]. Cambridge University Press (2022). [DOI 10.1017/9781009157926](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009157926); [official report page](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/). **Evidence type:** official global assessment. **Supports:** sensors, IoT, robotics, and AI can improve energy management/efficiency and low-emission technology adoption (high confidence), but demand growth can counterbalance gains and digital technology supports decarbonization only if appropriately governed (high confidence); digital divides, e-waste, resource pressures, and system rebound matter. **Boundary:** assessment of literature, not an AI-specific causal global abatement total.

24. **Victor Galaz; Miguel A. Centeno; Peter W. Callahan; Amar Causevic; Thayer Patterson; Irina Brass; Seth Baum; Darryl Farber; Joern Fischer; David Garcia; Timon McPhearson; Daniel Jimenez; Brian King; Paul Larcey; Karen Levy.** “Artificial intelligence, systemic risks, and sustainability.” *Technology in Society* 67, 101741 (2021). [DOI 10.1016/j.techsoc.2021.101741](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techsoc.2021.101741). **Evidence type:** global overview/risk framework. **Supports:** AI uptake in farming, forestry, and marine-resource extraction may create algorithmic/allocative harms, unequal access and benefits, cascading failures, external disruptions, and efficiency–resilience trade-offs that current governance incompletely addresses. **Boundary:** systemic-risk identification, not prevalence estimates or intervention effects.

25. **Aimee van Wynsberghe.** “Sustainable AI: AI for sustainability and the sustainability of AI.” *AI and Ethics* 1, 213–218 (2021). [DOI 10.1007/s43681-021-00043-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-021-00043-6). **Evidence type:** peer-reviewed opinion/conceptual paper. **Supports:** climate-beneficial applications (“AI for sustainability”) must be assessed alongside the environmental and social sustainability of the complete AI lifecycle; transparency and funding/governance incentives should address training and deployment costs. **Boundary:** normative framing, not empirical impact estimation.

26. **Parul Anand; Mark Coeckelbergh.** “AI, climate change and justice: elements for a normative framework centring the Global South.” *AI and Ethics* 6, Article 144 (2026). [DOI 10.1007/s43681-025-00949-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-025-00949-5). **Evidence type:** normative framework with India vignette. **Supports:** AI lifecycle impacts should be assessed through distributional and procedural justice, including who bears data-centre energy/water and mineral-extraction burdens and who participates in decisions; climate-vulnerable and data-poor communities risk exclusion. **Boundary:** normative/case-based argument, not a causal effectiveness study.

## AI’s own energy and carbon footprint

27. **Emma Strubell; Ananya Ganesh; Andrew McCallum.** “Energy and Policy Considerations for Deep Learning in NLP.” *Proceedings of the 57th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics*, 3645–3650 (2019). [DOI 10.18653/v1/P19-1355](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/P19-1355). **Evidence type:** empirical energy/carbon cost estimation. **Supports:** model development and hyperparameter search can multiply training energy/cost, and large-compute requirements create access inequities; motivates energy/carbon reporting and efficient research. **Boundary:** hardware, electricity mixes, workloads, and methods are 2019-specific; do not reuse its headline carbon numbers as estimates for present systems.

28. **Peter Henderson; Jieru Hu; Joshua Romoff; Emma Brunskill; Dan Jurafsky; Joelle Pineau.** “Towards the Systematic Reporting of the Energy and Carbon Footprints of Machine Learning.” *Journal of Machine Learning Research* 21(248), 1–43 (2020). [Stable JMLR URL](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v21/20-312.html) (no DOI assigned). **Evidence type:** measurement/reporting method plus case studies. **Supports:** direct, standardized energy and carbon reporting is feasible; measured real-time energy and location-specific carbon intensity are preferable to compute or thermal-design-power proxies alone. **Boundary:** framework/examples, not a sector-wide footprint estimate.

29. **Alexandra Sasha Luccioni; Sylvain Viguier; Anne-Laure Ligozat.** “Estimating the Carbon Footprint of BLOOM, a 176B Parameter Language Model.” *Journal of Machine Learning Research* 24(253), 1–15 (2023). [Stable JMLR URL](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v24/23-0069.html) (no DOI assigned). **Evidence type:** model-specific lifecycle accounting plus empirical deployment measurement. **Supports:** BLOOM final training was estimated at about 24.7 t CO2e for dynamic electricity alone and 50.5 t when equipment manufacture and operational processes were included, showing that system boundaries materially change totals; the study also measured inference. **Boundary:** one model, hardware site, and relatively low-carbon French grid; not representative of all foundation models.

30. **Alexandra Sasha Luccioni; Yacine Jernite; Emma Strubell.** “Power Hungry Processing: ⚡ Watts ⚡ Driving the Cost of AI Deployment?” *Proceedings of the 2024 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT ’24)*, 85–99 (2024). [DOI 10.1145/3630106.3658542](https://doi.org/10.1145/3630106.3658542). **Evidence type:** empirical inference-energy benchmark. **Supports:** across standardized 1,000-inference tests, multi-purpose generative architectures used orders of magnitude more energy than task-specific systems for several tasks even after controlling for parameter count; inference volume and model/task choice therefore matter alongside training. **Boundary:** sampled models, hardware, tasks, and carbon-intensity assumptions limit generalization.

## Claim-strength map for paper drafting

- **Observed/operational climate-relevant outcomes:** #7 (building energy field application); #14 (officially documented methane-alert/mitigation cases, without counterfactual aggregate attribution).
- **Empirical capability or forecast/MRV performance, not yet climate outcomes:** #12–13 and #16–18.
- **Life-cycle/scenario estimates:** #8–10 and #27–30. Label every number as a modeled scenario, accounting estimate, or benchmark; never call it observed global abatement.
- **Reviews/assessments/frameworks:** #1–6, #11, #15, #19–26. Use these for scope, mechanisms, limitations, and governance—not as direct intervention-effect estimates.

## Non-negotiable synthesis guardrails

1. Frame AI as an **enabling technology**, not an autonomous climate solution. Policy, physical infrastructure, finance, institutions, and behavior determine whether model improvements become real emissions reductions or avoided losses.
2. Keep a causal chain explicit: **prediction/detection improvement → operational decision → physical action → measured energy/emissions/loss outcome**. Most literature establishes only the first link.
3. Evaluate net impact using Kaack et al.’s three levels: computing footprint, immediate application effect, and system-level effects. Include induced demand/rebound and life-cycle boundaries.
4. Do not add sector potentials from IEA as if statistically independent; the 1,400 Mt value is already a scenario-wide aggregate and explicitly not a forecast.
5. Report geography, time period, baseline, uncertainty, hardware, measured kWh, electricity carbon intensity, and embodied hardware scope. Include water/material effects when data permit.
6. Validate across regions and climate regimes, especially data-scarce and vulnerable communities; include procedural participation and distribution of benefits/burdens.
7. Do not describe literature reproduction or secondary-data modeling as a new field experiment. Distinguish original computation from cited results and provide code/data provenance.
